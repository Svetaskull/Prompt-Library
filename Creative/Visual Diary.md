# Visual Diary

A personal generative AI project where thoughts, experiences and abstract emotional states are translated into visual concepts.

This project sits outside my commercial content work. There are no client briefs, brand guidelines or conversion goals here.

Instead, I use it as a space to experiment with visual storytelling, generative AI, art direction and image-to-video workflows.

The starting point can be a personal experience, a thought I want to give a visual form, a fictional character I enjoy, or simply an idea that feels worth exploring.

The goal is not to generate a beautiful image.

The goal is to find the right visual form for the idea.

---

## What I Explore

The project combines:

- Generative AI
- Visual storytelling
- Concept development
- Art direction
- Prompt iteration
- Image-to-video generation
- Symbolic imagery
- Dark fantasy and cinematic aesthetics
- Emotional storytelling
- Visual experimentation

Some concepts are personal.

Others begin with music, fictional worlds, characters, phrases or speculative ideas.

I do not try to force every image into a single narrative. The diary works more like a visual laboratory where different ideas can be explored without commercial constraints.

---

## My Process

I rarely treat prompting as a one-shot task.

A typical workflow looks more like this:

**Idea → Visual metaphor → Generation → Evaluation → Iteration → Selection → Animation**

### 1. Start with the idea

Sometimes the starting point is very specific.

Sometimes it is only a feeling.

Instead of immediately trying to write the perfect prompt, I first define what the image should communicate.

### 2. Find a visual metaphor

An abstract concept such as alienation, emotional instability or freedom is difficult to generate directly.

The important question becomes:

> What would this feeling look like if it existed physically?

This is where the concept begins turning into a scene.

### 3. Generate and evaluate

I test different compositions, environments, characters and visual directions.

A technically good image is not automatically a successful one.

I evaluate whether the result still communicates the original idea.

### 4. Iterate

If the image looks attractive but changes the meaning, I change direction.

This can involve:

- changing the character's posture;
- reducing or increasing visual intensity;
- changing clothing;
- removing unnecessary details;
- changing the environment;
- replacing the entire metaphor;
- switching AI models when another model interprets the concept better.

### 5. Animate selectively

Not every image needs movement.

When I use image-to-video generation, motion should strengthen the concept rather than simply make the image move.

Subtle breathing, camera movement, environmental motion, light changes or one deliberate character action can communicate more than a complicated animation.

# Case Studies

## 01 — I Knew You Would Destroy My Peace

### Starting Point

This concept came from a personal emotional experience: knowing that getting closer to someone could disturb an existing sense of stability and choosing to move closer anyway.

The central idea was not conventional romance.

It was attraction mixed with danger, hesitation and inevitability.

### Visual Problem

I wanted to show two people who are strongly drawn toward each other without making the scene feel soft, happy or traditionally romantic.

The visual needed to communicate:

- attraction;
- danger;
- emotional vulnerability;
- loss of control;
- hesitation;
- inevitability.

### First Attempt

I initially explored the concept in Midjourney.

The result had elements of the intended aesthetic but did not capture the emotional tension accurately enough.

Instead of continuing to force the same direction, I switched models.

### Final Image Direction

The static image was created with GPT Images.

The final metaphor became two people with glowing cracks across their skin.

The cracks represent instability and vulnerability, while the light makes the attraction feel almost physical.

They are extremely close, but the important part of the composition is the remaining distance between them.

That space represents the last moment in which they could still choose not to cross the line.

### Image-to-Video

The image was then animated in Kling.

The movement was intentionally restrained:

- slow movement toward each other;
- subtle breathing;
- small movements in hair and atmosphere;
- glowing cracks becoming brighter;
- gradual cinematic camera movement;
- eventual surrender to the attraction.

Instead of creating action for its own sake, the animation extends the original idea:

> They know getting closer may destroy their peace, and move closer anyway.

### What This Experiment Taught Me

The strongest motion is not always the most complex motion.

When the static image already contains emotional tension, animation works better when it amplifies that tension instead of replacing it with action.

## 02 — Digital Nomad

### Starting Point

This concept started with a real experience.

I spent several months travelling through Asia while continuing to work remotely.

That made me think about how ordinary remote work has become.

Working from another country once sounded unusual. Now it can simply mean opening a laptop somewhere else in the world.

So I pushed the idea further:

> What happens when a digital nomad is no longer choosing between countries, but between planets?

### Concept

The scene became a speculative version of remote work in the future.

A woman is travelling through space with her dog while continuing to work during the journey.

The spaceship is not primarily an adventure setting.

It is temporarily her home, office and means of transportation at the same time.

That distinction mattered.

### Iteration

Several early generations looked visually interesting but missed the intended feeling.

Some made the environment too futuristic.

Others made the character look like a spaceship crew member rather than an ordinary remote worker living in an extraordinary future.

This changed the direction of the iterations.

The goal became less:

> "Create a futuristic woman in space."

and more:

> "Create an ordinary remote-work moment in a future where interplanetary travel is normal."

That required balancing science-fiction elements with familiar details:

- comfortable clothing;
- a relaxed posture;
- a personal living space;
- headphones;
- a dog;
- a work interface;
- the view outside the spacecraft.

### Final Direction

The final composition treats space travel almost casually.

The extraordinary part of the scene exists outside the window.

Inside, the character is simply working.

That contrast became the central visual idea.

### Image-to-Video

The selected image was animated in Kling.

Again, the goal was not to turn the scene into an action sequence.

Small movements were more appropriate because the concept depends on normality:

a remote worker going about her day while travelling somewhere that would currently be unimaginably distant.

### What This Experiment Taught Me

Worldbuilding does not always require adding more futuristic details.

Sometimes the concept becomes more believable when extraordinary technology is surrounded by ordinary human behaviour.

## 03 — Alienation

### Starting Point

This concept originated from a period of emotional disconnection after returning home from a long trip.

I was physically present in familiar surroundings and conversations, but mentally felt somewhere else — participating in interactions without fully feeling part of them.

The initial request was extremely simple:

> "Can you generate alienation with a female character?"

The difficulty was discovering what *alienation* should actually look like.

### Finding the Visual Language

The concept gradually moved toward dark fantasy and the idea of withdrawal from the outside world.

Several visual metaphors were explored:

- isolation;
- a protective sphere;
- a dark-fantasy heroine;
- a ghost-like figure;
- a Lilith-inspired direction;
- a soul inside a cocoon.

Some iterations were aesthetically successful but conceptually wrong.

At one point, changing the character's clothing and pose made the image more sensual and visually polished.

But it stopped feeling like alienation.

The character began attracting the viewer's attention instead of withdrawing from it.

That became an important turning point.

### The Key Decision

The problem was not:

> "How do I make this image more beautiful?"

It was:

> "How do I preserve the psychological distance?"

The character needed to look as though she existed inside her own internal space.

Her posture became more closed.

The expression became distant rather than confident.

The surrounding darkness stopped functioning as an enemy she needed to defeat.

Instead, the outside world simply existed around her.

### Final Metaphor

The final direction became a pale figure enclosed inside a translucent cocoon while dark figures and hands remain outside.

The cocoon is not a superhero shield.

It represents psychological separation.

The outside world is close.

It can see her.

It can reach toward her.

But it cannot fully reach *her*.

### Animation

For the Kling animation, I deliberately kept the character almost still.

The outside world moves.

Shadows shift.

Hair moves slightly.

The cocoon subtly reacts.

The camera slowly approaches.

She remains withdrawn.

This inversion became important to the animation:

> The world continues moving around her while she remains somewhere inside herself.

### What This Experiment Taught Me

Visual quality and conceptual accuracy are different things.

A generation can become more attractive while becoming worse at communicating the original idea.

Learning when to reject a visually strong result is as important as learning how to generate one.

# Tools and Model Selection

I do not treat AI models as interchangeable generators.

Different tools are useful at different stages of the process.

### GPT Images

Useful when I need conversational iteration around a concept and want to progressively refine composition, character behaviour and symbolic details.

### Midjourney

Useful for visual exploration, atmosphere and discovering possible aesthetic directions.

It is not automatically the best model for every concept. If the visual style works but the emotional meaning does not, I switch tools rather than forcing the same workflow.

### Kling

Used for image-to-video experiments.

My main focus is usually controlled motion:

- subtle character movement;
- hair and fabric;
- atmospheric effects;
- light;
- slow camera movement;
- one meaningful action.

The objective is to preserve the composition and identity of the original image while adding another layer of storytelling.

---

# Failed Generations Are Part of the Process

I keep unsuccessful generations when they explain an important decision.

They help answer questions such as:

- Why did this composition fail?
- Why did the character feel wrong?
- Which detail changed the meaning?
- Was the problem technical or conceptual?
- Should the prompt change?
- Should the visual metaphor change?
- Would another model interpret the idea better?

This is one of the reasons I do not think of prompting as writing a single perfect command.

For me, it is closer to directing a visual process.

# Beyond Personal Experiences

Not every entry represents something happening in my life.

Sometimes I simply want to give an image to an idea.

A fictional character I enjoy can become the basis for a visual experiment.

A phrase can suggest a scene.

Music can create an atmosphere worth translating into an image.

A speculative question can turn into worldbuilding.

The diary intentionally allows all of these approaches to coexist.

What connects them is not autobiography.

It is the process of translating an idea into visual language.

# Why I Keep This Project

My commercial AI work usually begins with external constraints:

- brand requirements;
- product characteristics;
- target audience;
- platform format;
- content goals;
- visual guidelines.

The Visual Diary removes those constraints.

That gives me a place to experiment with concepts, models and workflows that may later influence commercial work.

It also allows me to practice something that prompt libraries alone cannot demonstrate:

**judgment.**

Generating an image is relatively easy.

Knowing why an image works, why another one does not, what should change and when a completely different direction is necessary is the more interesting part of the process.

---

## Key Principle

> Generative AI is not only a production shortcut.

It can also be a tool for visual thinking.

The prompt is part of the process.

The model is part of the process.

Iteration is part of the process.

But the final decision still depends on understanding what the image is supposed to communicate.
